Raising Emotionally Intelligent Kids: 5 Parenting Strategies That Work

Some months ago, I visited a family friend, and in the middle of our conversation, her 7-year-old son stormed into the living room, clearly upset.

“Mummy, I hate my sister! She took my crayons!” he shouted, arms crossed, tears brimming in his eyes.

His mum didn’t scold him or tell him to be quiet. Instead, she pulled him close and said, “I see that you’re upset. But let’s talk about this—why do you feel that way?”

Within minutes, the anger faded, and he was explaining his frustration rather than yelling.

That moment stuck with me.

Many kids struggle with emotions, not because they’re bad but because no one teaches them how to process their feelings in a good way.

And truth be told, most of us grew up hearing “Stop crying!” instead of “How do you feel?”

But times have changed.

Raising emotionally intelligent kids isn’t just a parenting trend—it’s a life skill that will help them thrive…

One thing you should understand is that, as a parent, you are your child’s first teacher. Before society or school teaches them anything, they first learn from you.

Good parenting creates healthy minds and confident kids who can stand up for themselves. It also shapes their morals and values.

There’s a popular saying in Nigeria: “Charity begins at home.” And I agree.

Good conduct starts at home.

A child who is raised with emotional intelligence will thrive faster and know how to treat others with love—because they first experienced it at home.

Every child is born with a clear and innocent mind. No child is born with thuggish traits or ill manners.

Whatever traits a child displays are learned from someone.

And often, that someone is the parent.

Good parenting produces emotionally healthy kids, and in turn, these kids grow to create a healthier society.

So, how can you raise a child who understands and manages emotions well?

Here are five strategies that work:

  1. Help Them Identify and Express Emotions

Many children know when they’re sad or angry, but they don’t always have the words to explain it. Instead of just saying, “Stop being grumpy!” try asking, “Are you feeling frustrated because you couldn’t finish your puzzle?”

When kids can name their emotions, they gain control over them. It’s like giving them a map to navigate their feelings

  1. Be Their Emotional Role Model

Children learn emotional intelligence by watching you. If you yell in frustration, they’ll think yelling is normal. If you handle stress by taking deep breaths, they’ll learn to do the same.

So the next time you’re overwhelmed, say, “I’m really tired right now. I need a moment to relax before we talk.” Trust me, they’ll copy that behavior.

  1. Teach Healthy Ways to Handle Big Emotions

Imagine your child is angry and about to throw something. Instead of shouting “Stop that!” (which rarely works), try this:

  • “You seem mad. Let’s take a deep breath together.”

  • “Would you like to talk about it or take a short walk to calm down?”

Giving them options helps them understand that emotions aren’t bad—they just need to be managed properly.

  1. Validate Their Feelings (Even When It’s Hard to Understand)

To you, it might seem silly that they’re crying over a broken toy, but to them, it’s a big deal. Saying “That’s nothing to cry about” doesn’t help—it only teaches them to suppress emotions.

Instead, say, “I know you’re sad because your toy broke. That must be disappointing. What can we do to make you feel better?”

When kids feel heard, they learn that their emotions are valid but can also be worked through.

  1. Encourage Empathy and Understanding

A simple way to teach empathy? Ask them how others feel.

If they push a friend on the playground, ask, “How do you think he felt when you did that?” If they share a snack, ask, “Did you notice how happy she was? How does that make you feel?”

Little by little, they’ll start seeing the world beyond their own emotions and become more compassionate.

Final Thoughts

Raising emotionally intelligent kids takes time, patience, and a lot of “Let’s try again” moments.

But imagine this: A child who grows up knowing how to manage anger, express sadness in words, and understand the feelings of others. That’s not just good parenting—it’s preparing them for life.

I hope we do better as parents
